How we looked at it

Logo Evaluation Framework

Every logo goes through the same five areas of evaluation — not style opinions, but functional checks based on common principles for how marks actually perform in the real world. Merch, signage, digital, print, one-color reproduction. A logo has to work across all of them.

  • Legibility — Can you read it everywhere it needs to be read?
  • Simplification — What survives when you strip it down — and does that core travel?
  • Art or Error — Does it hold together as a designed thing, or does it read as assembled?
  • Memory — Could someone reproduce the essence of this after seeing it once?
  • Belonging — Does it feel true to this specific brand — and only this brand?

Where you're winning

The concept is genuinely strong.

This is not a throwaway compliment. Two tests passed cleanly — and the two that passed are the hardest to manufacture. You can't design your way to a memorable concept. You either have one or you don't.

Memory — Pass
  • The crow-on-a-moon is immediately describable. "Black crow in front of a crescent moon" — that's a clear signature.
  • Not trend-dependent. This draws from a visual tradition with real longevity.
  • What people walk away remembering — the crow and moon — is genuinely ownable.
Belonging — Pass
  • This mark could not be swapped onto a competitor without immediate incongruity. It's specific.
  • Communicates something real: mystical, nature-rooted, slightly literary, independent.
  • The crow earns its place. Intelligence, luck, liminal spaces — it's the right symbol for what Lucky Crow is.
The signal in the data

Every element in this mark is there for a reason. The crow, the moon, the lotus (those lotus drinks are not an accident), the name. The concept is right. What the data is telling us is that the elements aren't yet in visual conversation with each other — they're sharing a frame without speaking the same language. That's a fixable execution problem, not a concept problem.

What needs work

Three fails. All of them functional.

None of these are aesthetic preferences. They're the places where the mark creates real costs — in production, in recognition, in how your audience reads it.

Fail
Legibility
Can you read it everywhere it needs to be read?
Where it breaks down
  • The brand name doesn't read at 1" wide in print or 80px wide on screen. At business card scale, the logo would need to fill the entire card to be legible — that's a production problem, not a logo spec.
  • "Apothecary & Readeasy" — the two descriptors that tell people what Lucky Crow actually is — disappear completely below 2" wide. They're doing real brand work and they're functionally invisible at most standard sizes.
  • Fine white detail strokes throughout the crow body drop below 2mm at standard sizes. That's the physical minimum a needle and thread can reproduce — embroidery is off the table in the current state.
  • The gold does not pass ADA digital contrast compliance against the dark background. It will fail accessibility checks in digital applications.
What's working
  • Fully vector — no raster elements. Clean foundation to build from.
  • Color count is controlled. The palette isn't the problem here.
Fail
Simplification
What survives when you strip it down — and does that core travel?
Where it breaks down
  • The crow illustration relies on black/white stroke contrast to read as a bird. Reproduce it in flat black and the concept doesn't hold — it reads as a solid shape. A mark that only works in full color isn't a logo, it's a color-dependent illustration.
  • The lotus has no simplified path. Embroidery, stamps, and one-color reproduction aren't viable without a redesign.
  • No simplified icon version exists. The full mark is being asked to do every job across every context — small sizes, merch, social profiles, favicon. That's too much load for one asset.
The seed of something good
  • The crow and moon composition is a strong anchor. In concept, this is ownable. The simplification work isn't about removing anything — it's about unlocking what's already there.
Fail
Art or Error
Does it hold together as a designed thing?
Where it breaks down
  • The business name is the most important piece of information in any logo. Right now it's the hardest thing to find. The crow dominates; the name gets buried. Hierarchy is inverted.
  • The crow and the lotus are drawn in different visual registers — different line weights, different levels of stylization, different attitudes. They're sharing a frame without speaking the same language. The mark reads as assembled rather than designed.
  • The crow sits between "real craft illustration" and "graphic mark" without fully committing to either. That middle ground is where the mark loses credibility. The audience in this space has taste — they'll feel it even if they can't name it.
The intention is clear
  • Every element earns its place conceptually. The crow, the moon, the lotus — this isn't random. It's a coherent visual world that needs its elements to align in execution, not a different concept.
  • Mystical, nature-rooted, slightly literary — the direction is right and the palette supports it consistently.

Where we go from here

The bones are here. Now build the system they deserve.

This isn't a start-over. Every element has a reason to be here. What's missing is the work that brings them into harmony — same visual language, proper hierarchy, a system that can scale.

01
Commit to a visual language across all elements

The crow, the lotus, the moon — they need to speak the same visual language. Same line weight, same level of stylization, same attitude. Right now they're in different registers. When they're unified, the mark reads as designed rather than assembled.

02
Rebalance the hierarchy

"Lucky Crow" is the most important piece of information in this mark. It needs to read at every size the logo gets used — not disappear behind the illustration. The name and the imagery need equal weight in the composition.

03
Build a simplified digital icon

The full mark does a lot of work. A simplified version — stripped down to the essential visual signature — gives Lucky Crow something that lives at small sizes, on social profiles, as a favicon, on merch. Not a replacement for the full mark. A complement to it.

04
Build a true one-color version

The current outline version only works on light backgrounds. A production-ready one-color mark — solid fills, no stroke dependency — opens up embroidery, screen print, stamps, and any reversed-out application. This is a gap the current system doesn't address.

The concept is right. The elements are right. The work ahead is getting them to move together.

Goals for the digital mark

What we're solving for.

Every decision in the iterations below is in service of these goals. Not redesign for its own sake — targeted fixes that make the mark do its job at the scale it needs to.

01
Pass at 110×110px

Social profile images display at approximately 110px on most platforms. That's the primary target. The mark needs to be immediately recognizable — brand name legible, key visual elements reading clearly — at that size without zooming in.

02
Visual balance between top and bottom halves

Right now the crow and moon carry the top half; the lotus and name band carry the bottom — but they're not in visual conversation. Bringing them into balance so the mark reads as a unified composition, not two elements stacked.

03
Cohesive illustration style

The crow and the lotus are currently drawn in different registers. The digital version works toward a consistent visual language across both — same weight, same stylization level, same attitude.

04
ADA-compliant contrast

Digital best practice, not optional. Colors need to pass contrast ratios for accessibility. This also makes the mark perform better at small scale — higher contrast = more legible at 110px.

05
Reduce to essential elements

At small scale, every element that isn't doing critical work becomes noise. The digital version strips back to what needs to be there and nothing more — so what remains can be read clearly.
